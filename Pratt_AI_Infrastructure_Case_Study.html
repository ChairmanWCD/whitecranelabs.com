01 — The context

A financial crisis forced a strategic choice

In 2024, Pratt entered a financial crisis that continues to the present. As the crisis became clear, the institution had to make a fundamental choice about how to maintain capability while reducing spending.

The traditional path—subscribing to ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, and other AI tools at institutional scale—was never going to be fiscally viable under budget pressure. Every seat costs money. Every tool costs money. At an institution with thousands of students and staff, that adds up quickly.

The question wasn't "Should we use AI?" It was: "How do we give our community access to cutting-edge AI tools without becoming hostage to subscription models that drain resources we don't have?"

We chose to build.

02 — The decision

Build vs. buy

Option A: Subscribe
ChatGPT Pro + Copilot + Claude ? $X per user per month
Multiply by thousands of students/staff
Unsustainable at institutional scale.
Additional costs: Quarterly switching costs as interfaces change. Lock-in risk. Data telemetry. Dependency on vendors' roadmaps.
Option B: Build
Infrastructure investment (one-time)
Staff retraining (sunk cost, already budgeted)
Ongoing maintenance: staff time
Usage-based billing. Institutional control.
Additional benefits: Model agnosticism. Data sovereignty. Speed. Ability to customize for institutional needs.

We chose Option B.

03 — The restructuring

What it actually cost to move fast

What we had

A Computer Lab team of 20+ people who managed the physical infrastructure of Pratt's computing ecosystem: lab management, printing systems, computer imaging, hardware and software support for classes. They were excellent at their work. The labs ran smoothly. They had deep institutional knowledge about what worked and what didn't.

What we asked them to do

Learn AI development. In months. With no prior training or knowledge. While maintaining their original responsibilities.

What actually happened

  • The Computer Lab team became the Advanced Technology team. Same people, new mandate. They had to learn enough AI to build, maintain, and support systems they'd never seen before.
  • Every campus department was asked to digitize years of institutional knowledge in months. Some did it well. Some didn't. Some knowledge is still being converted from analog to digital.
  • The elite AI team was formed. Select individuals from the Advanced Technology team showed aptitude and were pulled to work on AI development full-time. The rest maintained the old infrastructure while learning new skills.
  • The reality wasn't clean. There were conflicts. There was friction. Leadership guidance was unclear at times. People were overloaded. But the people adapted. They learned. They built.

What it actually costs to move fast is honesty about the cost.

The overload on staff was real. This wasn't the ideal way to do it. In a perfect world, you'd hire new people specifically for AI development and let the existing team focus on their original work. But the institution didn't have that budget. So we asked the team to do more. They did. And it worked. But it's not sustainable long-term without either additional hires, process optimization, or explicit reprioritization of what matters most.

That's the part worth documenting: what it actually costs to move fast under constraint.

04 — What we built

Three systems, one mission

llm.pratt.edu

A single interface to multiple LLM ecosystems. Students and staff access the same tool. No subscription friction. No vendor lock-in.

  • Proprietary models: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Xai. Users can route the same query to different models and compare results.
  • Open-source models: DeepSeek, Gemma. Installed locally on Pratt infrastructure—data never leaves campus.
  • Custom models: Built and fine-tuned for specific departments. When a department has specialized needs or sensitive information, we assess security requirements and build a model (API-connected or locally hosted) specifically for them.
Example: Athletics Department Bot
Trained on team rosters, game stats, season history, and game schedules. Handles student/staff questions: "When's the next game?" "What are this team's stats?" "Who are the starters?" Reduces the manual inquiry load on athletics staff. Runs on Pratt infrastructure—no data leaves campus.

ai.pratt.edu

Generative tools interface. Image generation, code generation, document creation, analysis. Same tools available to all. No approval bottleneck. Built and maintained by the elite AI team.

Pratt Knowledge System

Internal knowledge base built by the AI team. Answers institutional questions about policies, registration, procedures, deadlines. When the system gets it wrong, staff flag it, and the team retrains the model. This is human-in-the-loop in practice—the system handles routine questions, humans maintain judgment on what matters.

Department-specific models

When a department has specialized knowledge or sensitive information, we don't force them into a generic system. We build for their needs.

06 — The culture

How we maintained institutional autonomy

Freedom of choice, not mandate

We built systems and made them available. We recommend using Pratt's tools—they're current, they're well-built, they're free.

But if a faculty member wants to pay for ChatGPT? That's their choice. We don't deny it. We don't police it. If you're a student and you prefer a different tool, go use it. The community decides what's best for them.

Respect for legitimate concerns

A lot of students and faculty worry that AI will:

  • Displace jobs in industries that depend on creative work
  • Have ecological impact (energy consumption, data centers, water usage for cooling)
  • Create economic inequality (who benefits from AI? who bears the cost?)

These are legitimate concerns. We don't dismiss them. We don't pretend they don't exist.

What we do:

  • Monitor usage: Make sure we're not amplifying waste. Pay-as-you-go means we're optimizing, not sustaining growth for growth's sake.
  • Be transparent: Everything that goes in and out of our AI systems is tracked and reported. Faculty and students can see what the systems are doing.
  • Support choice: If you don't want to use AI, you don't have to. No requirement. No shame.
  • Create space for skepticism: The conversation about AI's impact continues in classrooms, in studios, in meetings. Not just in IT.

Decision authority stays with the community

We provide the tool. You decide if it's right for you. Some faculty integrate AI into courses. Some don't. Some students use it daily. Some use it once. Some don't use it at all.

That hasn't changed. The institution provides choice. The community decides.

07 — What's still hard

The tradeoffs we're still managing

01

Staff overload is not sustainable long-term. The Advanced Technology team is carrying more than their original responsibilities. They're good at it. But it's not sustainable without either additional hires, process optimization, or explicit prioritization of what matters most. We haven't solved this yet. That's honest.

02

Knowledge digitization was aggressive. We tried to convert years of institutional knowledge in months. Some departments did it well. Some didn't. Some knowledge is still analog. We tried to do everything at once and learned that's not realistic.

03

Building is harder than buying. It's cheaper at scale, but it requires technical sophistication, ongoing maintenance, and institutional commitment. You can't be entirely hands-off. But that's the point—institutions should own their tools.

08 — Why this matters

The principle

We treat AI not as a product to purchase but as a capability to cultivate—in a way that respects community autonomy, acknowledges legitimate concerns, and measures what actually matters to the institution.

For other institutions:

If you're facing budget constraints and want AI capability, this is a playbook:

  1. Assess what you have (existing technical staff, infrastructure)
  2. Identify what you need (capability, not products)
  3. Invest in people (retraining > hiring)
  4. Build with institutional constraints in mind (security, privacy, sustainability)
  5. Maintain community autonomy (choice, not mandate)

For leadership:

Building capability under constraint requires speed, honesty about tradeoffs, respect for institutional culture, and measurement that matters. It's not about executing perfectly. It's about moving forward while being honest about what's hard.

What this proves:

During a financial crisis, you can build more—not less. You can give your community access to cutting-edge tools. You can do it sustainably. You can do it while maintaining culture and respecting community autonomy.

The cost is clarity about what you're optimizing for and honesty about the tradeoffs involved in getting there.
