Case Study · Multi-Agent AI · Education
The Critique
Council

A multi-agent AI system designed to simulate the one thing design education rarely gives students enough of: practice presenting to a jury of people who don't agree.

Built
2024 · Updated stack 2026
Stack
Python · OpenAI · Anthropic · Google · ElevenLabs · Gradio
Pillar
Creative Infrastructure · AI × Design · Creative Culture
01 — The problem
Design schools prepare students for the work. Not for the room.

Design school critiques are one of the highest-stakes moments in a student's education — and one of the least practiced. Students arrive underprepared, receive feedback from a single professor's perspective, and have no way to rehearse presenting their work to a jury of people with different standards, agendas, and levels of patience.

The gap isn't technical skill. It's the ability to hear criticism from multiple directions simultaneously and defend your decisions under pressure. That's a learned skill. The Critique Council was built to train it.

The multi-agent design isn't a technical flourish. It's a pedagogy decision. Real juries don't agree.
02 — System architecture
Five integrated AI layers. One coherent educational experience.

The system routes a student's artwork and an optional focus question through a shared JSON rubric, then fires three distinct critic agents concurrently. Each agent processes the same image through its persona lens — returning structured feedback with dynamic critique categories determined by what it actually observes, not generic buckets.

03 — Agent design decisions
Why three agents. Why these models. Why these personas.

The most important design decision in the system has nothing to do with code. It's the choice to build intentional disagreement into the architecture. A single AI critic would flatten feedback into a consensus that doesn't exist in real professional critique. Three distinct personas — with different standards, different tones, and different levels of patience — train students to navigate something closer to reality.

GPT-5 mini
Agent 01
Student critic

Peer-level feedback. Constructive, encouraging, focused on shared learning. Uses the efficient end of the GPT-5 family — fast response, accessible language. The voice students read first and find easiest to absorb.

Claude Opus 4.5
Agent 02
Professor critic

Balanced authority. Concept and technique held in tension. Opus 4.5 was chosen specifically for its ability to reason about tradeoffs without hand-holding — which is what a studio professor with decades of experience actually does.

Gemini 3 Pro
Agent 03
Industry critic

No filter. Direct. Contextual analysis over encouragement. Gemini 3 Pro's multimodal depth means it reads the image with genuine visual reasoning — making its harsh feedback specific and defensible rather than generic.

Nano Banana 2 · ElevenLabs
Output layer
Voice + visual feedback

ElevenLabs replaces generic TTS — each critic's persona has a distinct vocal character. Nano Banana 2 generates before/after visual examples directly from critique action items at 4K quality and Flash speed.

04 — Technical stack
What's running under the hood.
GPT-5 mini Claude Opus 4.5 Gemini 3 Pro Nano Banana 2 ElevenLabs TTS Python · asyncio Gradio UI PIL · aiohttp JSON schema Response cache layer

Three agents run concurrently via asyncio.gather() — keeping total response time tolerable for classroom use regardless of which critics are selected. A cache layer deduplicates on image hash + prompt, preventing redundant API calls when students iterate on the same piece.

05 — Honest lessons
What I'd do differently. What I learned I couldn't predict.

This section exists because most portfolios skip it. That's a mistake — the lessons section is where a Director-level practitioner separates from a developer showing off a project.

01

The image generation layer is the most brittle. DALL-E 3 prompts built from critique text are noisy — the distance between "action item extracted from JSON" and "useful educational image" is longer than expected. Nano Banana 2's world knowledge grounding closes this gap significantly, but prompt engineering the visual feedback layer still requires more iteration than any other part of the system.

02

Voice differentiation has more pedagogical impact than expected. When each critic sounds like a different person, students engage with the feedback differently. The TTS upgrade from OpenAI to ElevenLabs isn't cosmetic — it's the feature that makes the persona architecture feel real rather than mechanical.

03

JSON parsing failures require graceful fallbacks. When the API doesn't return clean structured output — which happens — the system needs a regex fallback to extract action items. Relying solely on JSON parsing creates failure modes that surface in front of students at the worst moments.

04

This system simulates critique. It cannot replace it. The live negotiation of a real jury — the follow-up question, the silence, the defense — is beyond what any AI panel can replicate today. The Critique Council is a rehearsal tool, not a substitute. That ceiling is a feature, not a failure. Being honest about what AI can and cannot do is part of the design philosophy here.

06 — What I'd build differently today
The architecture is the same. The ceiling is higher.

The original Critique Council was built on the best available models in early 2024. The concept has held. The stack has been superseded. A rebuilt version routes the student agent through GPT-5's efficient tier for speed, assigns the professor role to Claude Opus 4.5 for its depth of reasoning and tolerance for ambiguity, gives the industry critic to Gemini 3 Pro for its multimodal visual analysis, replaces DALL-E 3 with Nano Banana 2 for faster and higher-fidelity educational imagery, and upgrades the voice layer from OpenAI TTS to ElevenLabs for persona-specific emotional range.

Tracking what's changed — and why the choices matter — is what a Director of Creative Technology is paid to do. The stack update is the portfolio update.
